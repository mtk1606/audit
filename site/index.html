What Happens When Avellaneda-Stoikov Meets a Real Order Queue?

An empirical audit of queue position, adverse selection, and optimal market making.

  1. What a market maker does It keeps offering to buy slightly below the market price and sell slightly above it, earning the difference. Its risk is being left holding shares when the price moves.
  2. What Avellaneda-Stoikov solves A well-known 2008 model (AS for short) that tells a market maker where to set those two prices, based on how many shares it already holds and how much risk it will take.
  3. What the queue is On a real exchange, orders at the same price wait in line. The order that arrived first is filled first.
  4. Why it could break the model AS assumes every order at a given price has the same chance of being filled. If your place in line matters more than your price, the model's advice can be wrong in ways it cannot see.

I set out to measure that. First I reproduced the paper's published results, which meant identifying a rule the paper never states. Then I built and tested a simulator that can switch the queue, and two other missing effects, on and off one at a time.

Where it stands: the replication is complete and the simulator is tested. The real-market experiment has not run yet, and nothing on this page claims its result.

01The idea

Two orders at the same price are not the same order

Picture a line at a counter where everyone wants to buy at $100.00. When a seller arrives, the person at the front is served first. If you joined late and the seller only has a few shares, they run out before they reach you.

Real exchanges work like that line. The AS model does not have one. It gives every buyer at $100.00 the same chance of being filled, whether they are first or fortieth.

02The question

I wanted to know whether AS quotes lose money for reasons the model cannot see, and how much each reason costs

The model leaves out three things every market maker deals with. I label them A1, A2 and A3 throughout.

The three effects the AS model leaves out
EffectThe model assumesIn a real market
A1 Queue positionYour chance of a fill depends only on how far your price is from the market priceIt depends on how many shares are ahead of you at that price
A2 Adverse selectionBeing filled tells you nothing about where the price goes nextFills tend to arrive just before the price moves against you
A3 CompetitionYou quote alone and nobody reactsOther traders join or cancel when your order changes the line

My hypothesis. Once queue position and adverse selection are present, AS systematically misprices its quotes. The cost can be measured, split between the three effects, and partly recovered with a corrected rule.

What would count as success. A cost for each effect, in basis points (hundredths of a percent of the value traded), with confidence intervals, holding up across five stocks.

What would count against it. Small costs, or results whose direction depends on the cancellation assumption. I decided in advance to publish that outcome as prominently as a positive one.

Designed so it can fail

The replication test, its three candidate rules and its pass mark were committed before any number was computed5.

For the real-market experiment, a pre-registration is drafted. It must be committed and tagged before any real-market result is computed. It is not yet in force.

03The model

How Avellaneda-Stoikov sets its quotes

The model does two things. When you already hold shares, it lowers both prices so you are more likely to sell, and when you are short it raises them. It also sets how far apart the two prices are, wider when you are more cautious or have more time left.

Try it with the paper's own settings. At the highest risk setting and a large position, the model asks for a chance of being filled that is above 100%, which is impossible. The paper does not say what its simulation did then, and that gap is where my replication work started.

Technical detail: the equations and the simulation rule

Reservation price (the model's inventory-adjusted fair price) and total spread, published equations 29 and 30, with inventory q, risk aversion γ, volatility σ and remaining time T − t:

r(s, q, t) = s − q·γ·σ²·(T − t) δa + δb = γ·σ²·(T − t) + (2/γ)·ln(1 + γ/k) bid, ask = r ∓ (δa + δb)/2 λ(δ) = A·exp(−k·δ)

The numerical experiment runs 1,000 paths of 200 steps2. Each step, the bid and ask fill independently with probability λ(δ)·dt at the current quotes, then the mid moves by ±σ√dt. The symmetric benchmark quotes the time-averaged AS spread around the mid. Profit is terminal cash plus inventory marked at the terminal mid.

The unstated case: when λ(δ)·dt > 1, a Bernoulli probability is undefined. In my first seeded runs this happened for the inventory strategy in Tables 1 and 3, with raw values up to 7.593.

The full Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman derivation, including the exponential-utility transformation and the frozen-inventory approximation, is in docs/DERIVATION.md.

05What I found

Research status

  • CompleteOriginal-paper replication. All three published tables reproduced under one identified rule4.
  • ValidatedSimulator correctness. Reduces exactly to the paper's model; market-mechanics tests pass on synthetic order flow6.
  • Synthetic onlyEnd-to-end measurement pipeline. Validated on synthetic test data. Its economic outputs are not findings8.
  • ImplementedCorrected policy. Derived and implemented; reduces to AS when both corrections are switched off7.
  • PendingReal-market attribution (measuring each effect's cost on real data). Not run.
  • PendingHeld-out real-market economic result. Not run; the held-back data is untouched.

The published results come from a capped fill rule and the paper's time step

  • I reproduced all three published tables, under one specific reading of the paper.When the chance of a fill is capped at 100%, all 27 printed values sit within the range chance alone would produce. The largest gap is 1.88 standard errors; I had fixed the cut-off at 3.11 in advance4.
  • The natural alternative rule fails.Treating fills as random arrivals, 1 − exp(−λ·dt), puts 7 of 27 values outside that range, one of them 38.5 standard errors away. It lowers expected profit by about 12% in Table 14.
  • The numbers belong to the paper's time step, not to the continuous-time model.With the same capped rule, halving the time step puts 6 values outside the range, and a five-times finer step puts 11 outside4.
  • The paper does not state the capping rule. The evidence supports it; it is my inference, not the authors' documented procedure.

How I tested it. Comparing the paper with one random simulation of my own would compare noise with noise. Instead I calculated each candidate model's exact expected results, including how much they vary. Then I asked whether each printed number is a plausible outcome of the paper's own 1,000-path experiment2. The test, the three candidate rules and the cut-off were committed before anything was computed5.

The simulator reduces to this result

With all three switches off, my simulator should behave exactly like the paper's model. It does: across four configurations of 1,500 paths each, its profit and inventory statistics match the exact capped-rule results within 4.5 standard errors6. That test connects every later experiment back to the paper.

I derived a corrected rule, and it disagreed with my own spec

Adding queue-dependent fills and an expected adverse price move to the model's optimisation gives a rule that reduces to AS when both additions are off. The derivation put the adverse-selection term with the opposite sign to the formula my specification had guessed: the side with more adverse selection is quoted further away7. I kept the derivation. The rule has not been evaluated on market data.

06What failed

Where the evidence pushed back

Every one of these results is kept in the repository. None of the original criteria was loosened to make a test pass.

Negative result

My first replication did not pass

What happened
My first test compared the paper with one seeded simulation. Tables 1 and 3 could not even run under a strict reading: the model asked for fill chances up to 7.59 (759%), with 10,259 such cases in the Table 3 run3.
What I changed
I did not search for a lucky random seed. I built the exact-calculation test above, fixed its rules and cut-off first, and let the data choose between them.
Outcome
The original failure is preserved and still reported as "not reproduced" under its own terms3.
Negative result

A pass mark from my own spec was too strict

What happened
My spec required one headline ratio to land within 10% of the paper. In Table 1 the printed ratio (3.70) is 14.2% from the exact ratio (4.23), yet only 1.64 standard errors away4.
Why it matters
The paper's own sampling error on that ratio is about 8%4. A ±10% band would reject the true model often.
Outcome
I documented it as a proposed amendment, not a silent change.
Synthetic only

The measurement pipeline ran, and its numbers mean nothing yet

What happened
I ran the full measurement on 5 synthetic stocks to test the machinery. The benchmark rule it compares against converged in 0 of 560 fits, because I gave its search a deliberately tiny budget of 30 evaluations8.
Why it matters
An under-fitted benchmark makes AS's measured shortfall meaningless. The competition effect also changed direction between the cancellation extremes, so the pipeline withheld it, as designed8.
Outcome
None of those economic values appears on this page. The real-market configuration treats non-convergence as a hard failure.
Blocked

The real-market run has not happened

What happened
The LOBSTER files were checked in an earlier environment1. The environment where I built the simulator could not reach the data host15.
Outcome
Every simulator and measurement result is on synthetic order flow. My starting hypothesis is still untested.
07Engineering

A replay engine where fills have to be earned

The simulator replays the order book in 100-millisecond batches12. Each batch runs the same six steps, and any of the three effect models can be swapped without touching the loop.

Fill mechanics

  • Trades at my price use up the shares ahead of me first.
  • A large order that trades through my price fills me only up to its own size.
  • A repriced order goes to the back of its new line.
  • An order the market moves through is suspended, not filled at an impossible price.
  • Hidden-order trades never fill me. That can only lower fill counts.

Modelling decisions

  • Adverse selection is added only where the fill model does not already contain it, so every combination of A1 and A2 means the same thing.
  • Competition acts through cancellations ahead of me, scaled by how strongly other traders were measured to react to depth.
  • Prices are whole ticks and timestamps whole nanoseconds.
  • Every statistical fit that fails to converge raises an error.

Research plumbing

  • Every run writes a manifest: git commit, configuration fingerprint, input checksums, seeds, environment.
  • Runs refuse uncommitted code unless the exception is recorded.
  • Random seeds come from explicit keys per stock, episode and strategy.
  • Parallel workers change speed, never results.

08Correctness

What the tests are there to catch

The suite has 146 passing tests, plus linting and strict type checking9. The count matters less than what each group would catch.

Exact mathematics

  • The exact calculation matches brute-force enumeration of every path.Catches a wrong formula for how much results vary.
  • The symmetric strategy matches its closed form.Catches probability leaking out of the calculation.

Reduction to the paper

  • With all switches off, the simulator matches the paper's model exactly.Catches a simulator that drifts from the model it claims to test.
  • The corrected rule reduces to AS with both corrections off.Catches a correction that changes the baseline.

Market mechanics (property tests)

  • Cash plus inventory at the mid-price equals the profit breakdown at every step.Catches accounting drift.
  • No fill occurs through the other side of the book.Catches impossible trades.
  • Queue position only improves until a reprice, which goes to the back.Catches fills that were never earned.
  • Fill counts order as all-behind ≤ spread-evenly ≤ all-ahead.Catches bounds that do not bound.

Determinism

  • Fixed seeds give byte-identical fill sequences, checked against stored fingerprints.Catches silent behaviour changes.
  • All eight worlds share the same random numbers.Catches the seeding bug below.
  • Worker count does not change any output.Catches order-dependent parallel code.
  • A fresh one-command run from a clean checkout reproduced my committed synthetic run byte for byte14.Catches hidden state between runs.

Research guards

  • The three effects' contributions always sum to the total.Catches a broken decomposition.
  • Held-back episodes are never replayed by the measurement.Catches data leakage.
  • The held-back evaluation refuses a second run unless a reason is recorded.Catches quiet re-testing.

GitHub Actions runs linting, strict type checking and the full test suite on every push. All 8 runs up to commit 473fedd passed9.

09Bugs I caught

Two bugs that would have produced believable wrong answers

Different random numbers in every world

Symptom
None visible. Every run completed normally.
Why it invalidated the experiment
The measurement compares the eight worlds against each other. With different random streams, chance differences would have been reported as the cost of queue position or adverse selection.
Root cause
NumPy's SeedSequence.spawn() advances an internal counter. Calling it once per run gave each world a different random stream.
Fix
Seeds now come from explicit keys per stock, episode and strategy, identical in every world13.
Regression protection
A test checks that two worlds which differ only in an inactive switch produce identical fills. The old code fails it13.

Five stocks, one name

Symptom
The log of my first full synthetic run listed every session under the same name, SYN.
Why it invalidated the experiment
Results are filed by stock and episode, so the five sessions overwrote each other. The output looked complete but mixed records from different stocks.
Root cause
The synthetic data generator hard-coded the name.
Fix
The name is passed through, and loading now rejects duplicate names. I discarded the run and recorded why in the decision log13.
Regression protection
A pipeline test asserts that each stock is measured separately13.

13Next

The decisive experiment

Turning the tested machinery into evidence takes four steps, in this order:

  1. Replay one real LOBSTER day through all eight worlds. Measure the real replay time, and confirm the market-mechanics tests hold on real order flow.
  2. Commit and tag the pre-registration before computing any real-market result.
  3. Measure all five stocks with a search budget large enough for the benchmark rule to converge. Report adverse selection first, with the cancellation extremes.
  4. Only if those results are stable, open the held-back data once to test the corrected rule.
