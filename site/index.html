Market microstructure · Avellaneda-Stoikov (2008) audit

What happens when a famous market-making model meets a real order queue?

The Avellaneda-Stoikov model tells a market maker where to place its buy and sell quotes. It assumes a quote's chance of being filled depends only on how far it sits from the market price, as if nobody else were waiting at that price. I wanted to measure what that assumption costs. I first reproduced the paper's published results, which meant identifying a numerical rule the paper never states. Then I built a simulator where queue position, adverse selection and competition can each be switched on and off.

Where it stands: the replication and the simulator are verified. The real-market attribution experiment is the step that remains, and nothing on this page claims its result.

01The idea

Two orders at the same price are not the same order

Picture a line at a counter where everyone wants to buy at $100.00. When a seller arrives, the person at the front is served first. If you joined late and the seller only has a few shares, they run out before they reach you.

Real exchanges work like that line. The Avellaneda-Stoikov model does not have one. It gives every buyer at $100.00 the same chance of being filled, whether they are first or fortieth.

02The question

I wanted to know whether the model's quotes lose money for reasons it cannot see, and how much each reason costs

The model leaves out three things that every market maker deals with:

AxisThe model assumesIn a real market
A1 Queue positionFill chance depends only on distance from the mid-priceFill chance depends on how much volume is ahead of you at that price
A2 Adverse selectionBeing filled tells you nothing about the next price moveFills tend to arrive just before the price moves against you
A3 CompetitionYou quote alone and nobody reactsOther participants join or cancel when your order changes the depth

My hypothesis. The AS reservation price is systematically biased once queue position and adverse selection are present. The bias can be measured and split by assumption, and a corrected rule can recover part of it.

What would count as success. An attribution in basis points for each axis, with bootstrap confidence intervals and cancellation bounds, stable across five symbols.

What would count against it. Small attributions, or bounds that straddle zero. I decided in advance to publish that outcome with the same prominence as a positive one.

Designed so it can fail

The replication test, its three candidate rules and its pass threshold were committed before any number was computed (commit 6adb9a3).

For the market experiment, a pre-registration is drafted. It must be committed and tagged before any market-data attribution is computed. It is not yet in force.

03The model

How Avellaneda-Stoikov sets its quotes

The model does two things. When you already hold inventory, it shifts both quotes so you are more likely to sell when long and buy when short. It also widens the quotes when you are more risk averse or have more time left, and narrows them when fills fall off quickly with distance.

Try it with the paper's own parameters. At high risk aversion and a large position, the model asks for a per-step fill probability above one. The paper does not say what its simulation did then, and that gap is where my replication work started.

Technical detail: the equations and the simulation rule

Reservation price and total spread (published equations 29 and 30), with inventory q, risk aversion γ, volatility σ and remaining time T − t:

r(s, q, t) = s − q·γ·σ²·(T − t) δa + δb = γ·σ²·(T − t) + (2/γ)·ln(1 + γ/k) bid, ask = r ∓ (δa + δb)/2 λ(δ) = A·exp(−k·δ)

The numerical experiment runs 1,000 paths of 200 steps. Each step, the bid and ask fill independently with probability λ(δ)·dt at the current quotes, then the mid moves by ±σ√dt. The symmetric benchmark quotes the time-averaged AS spread around the mid. Profit is terminal cash plus inventory marked at the terminal mid.

The unstated case: when λ(δ)·dt > 1, a Bernoulli probability is undefined. In my first seeded runs this happened on the inventory strategy in Tables 1 and 3, with raw values up to 7.59.

The full HJB derivation, including the exponential-utility transformation and the frozen-inventory approximation, is in docs/DERIVATION.md.

05What I found

The published results come from a capped fill rule and the paper's time step

  • I reproduced all three published tables, under one specific reading of the paper.Every one of the 27 printed values is statistically plausible when fill probabilities are capped at one. The largest deviation is 1.88 standard errors, against a threshold of 3.11.
  • The natural alternative rule fails.Treating fills as Poisson arrivals, 1 − exp(−λ·dt), puts 7 of 27 values outside the band, one of them 38.5 standard errors away. It lowers expected profit by about 12% in Table 1.
  • The numbers belong to the paper's time step, not to the continuous-time model.With the same capped rule, halving the step puts 6 values outside the band, and a five-times finer step puts 11 outside.
  • The paper does not state the capping rule. The evidence supports it; it is my inference, not the authors' documented procedure.

How I tested it. Comparing the paper with one random simulation of my own would compare noise with noise. Instead I computed each model's exact expected profit and inventory, including the fourth moments needed for the spread of a standard deviation. Then I asked whether each printed number is a plausible outcome of the paper's own 1,000-path experiment. The test, the three candidate rules and the threshold were committed before anything was computed.

The simulator reduces to this result

With all three switches off, my event-driven simulator should behave exactly like the paper's model. It does: across four configurations and 1,500 paths each, its profit and inventory statistics match the exact capped-rule moments within 4.5 standard errors. That is the test that connects every later experiment back to the paper.

I derived a corrected rule, and it disagreed with my own spec

Adding queue-dependent fill intensity and an expected adverse move to the model's optimisation gives a rule that reduces exactly to AS when both terms are off. The derivation put the adverse-selection term with the opposite sign to the formula my specification had guessed: the side with more adverse selection is quoted further away. I kept the derivation. The rule has not been evaluated on market data.

06What failed

Where the evidence pushed back

I kept every one of these results in the repository. None of the original criteria was loosened to make a test pass.

Negative result

My first replication did not pass

What happened
The original contract compared the paper with one seeded simulation. Tables 1 and 3 could not even run under a strict reading, because the model asked for fill probabilities up to 7.59, with 10,259 such cases in the Table 3 run.
What I changed
I did not tune seeds. I built the exact-moment test above, fixed its rules and threshold first, and let the data choose between them.
Outcome
The original failure is preserved and still reported as "not reproduced" under its own contract.
Negative result

A criterion from my own spec was too strict

What happened
My spec required the variance ratio within 10% of the paper. In Table 1 the printed ratio (3.70) is 14.2% from the exact ratio (4.23), yet only 1.64 standard errors away.
Why it matters
The paper's own sampling error on that ratio is about 8%. A ±10% band would reject the true model often.
Outcome
I documented it as a proposed amendment, not a silent change.
Synthetic only

The attribution pipeline ran, and its numbers mean nothing yet

What happened
I ran the full attribution on 5 synthetic symbols to test the machinery. The benchmark policy converged in 0 of 560 fits, because I gave it a deliberately tiny search budget of 30 evaluations.
Why it matters
An under-fitted benchmark makes the AS shortfall meaningless. The competition axis also changed sign between cancellation bounds, so the pipeline withheld its headline, as designed.
Outcome
I do not report those economic values anywhere. The market-data configuration treats non-convergence as a hard failure.
Blocked

The market-data run has not happened

What happened
The LOBSTER files were validated in an earlier environment. The environment where I built the simulator could not reach the data host.
Outcome
Every simulator and attribution result is on synthetic order flow. My starting hypothesis is still untested.
07Engineering

A replay engine where fills have to be earned

The simulator replays the order book in 100-millisecond batches. Each batch runs the same six steps, and any of the three models can be swapped without touching the loop.

Fill mechanics

  • Executions at my price use up the volume ahead of me first.
  • A sweep through my price fills me only up to its own volume.
  • A repriced order goes to the back of its new queue.
  • An order the market moves through is suspended, not filled at a crossed price.
  • Hidden executions never fill me. That can only lower fill counts.

Modelling decisions

  • A2 is booked as a target minus what the fill model already embeds, so all four A1 × A2 cells mean the same thing.
  • A3 acts through cancellations ahead of me, scaled by a fitted depth elasticity.
  • Prices are integer ticks and timestamps integer nanoseconds.
  • Every calibration fit that fails to converge raises an error.

Research plumbing

  • Every run writes a manifest: git commit, config hash, input checksums, seeds, environment.
  • Runs refuse a dirty git tree unless the exception is recorded.
  • Seeds come from explicit keys per symbol, episode and strategy.
  • Parallel workers change speed, never results.

08Correctness

What the tests are there to catch

The suite has 143 passing tests, with linting and strict type checking. The count matters less than what each group would catch.

Exact mathematics

  • The moment recurrence matches brute-force enumeration of every path.Catches a wrong variance or kurtosis formula.
  • The symmetric strategy matches its closed form.Catches probability mass leaking out of the state space.

Reduction to the paper

  • With all switches off, the simulator matches the exact paper law.Catches a simulator that drifts from the model it claims to test.
  • The corrected rule reduces to AS with both terms off.Catches a correction that changes the baseline.

Market mechanics (property tests)

  • Cash plus inventory at mid equals the P&L decomposition at every step.Catches accounting drift.
  • No fill occurs through the opposite side of the book.Catches impossible trades.
  • Queue position only improves until a reprice, which goes to the back.Catches optimistic fills.
  • Pessimistic ≤ spread-evenly ≤ optimistic fill counts.Catches bounds that do not bound.

Determinism

  • Fixed seeds give byte-identical fill sequences, checked against stored hashes.Catches silent behaviour changes.
  • Grid worlds share random numbers.Catches the seeding bug below.
  • Worker count does not change any output.Catches order-dependent parallel code.
  • A fresh one-command run from a clean checkout reproduced my committed synthetic attribution run byte for byte.Catches hidden state between runs.

Research guards

  • Shapley values sum to the total gap for every episode.Catches a broken decomposition.
  • Holdout episodes are never replayed by the attribution run.Catches data leakage.
  • The holdout evaluation refuses a second run unless a reason is recorded.Catches quiet re-testing.

A nightly CI workflow runs the gate and the full reproduction. It is configured but has not yet run on GitHub.

09Bugs I caught

Two bugs that would have produced believable wrong answers

Different random numbers in every world

Symptom
None visible. Every run completed normally.
Root cause
NumPy's SeedSequence.spawn() advances an internal counter. Calling it once per run gave each of the eight worlds a different random stream.
Risk
The attribution compares worlds pairwise. With different streams, sampling noise would have been reported as an effect of queue position or adverse selection.
Fix
Seeds now come from explicit keys per symbol, episode and strategy, identical in every world.
Protection
A regression test checks that two worlds which differ only in an inactive switch produce identical fills. The old derivation fails it.

Five symbols, one name

Symptom
The log of my first full synthetic run listed every session as SYN.
Root cause
The synthetic generator hard-coded the symbol. Results are keyed by symbol and episode, so the five sessions overwrote each other.
Risk
A complete-looking attribution built from mixed records.
Fix
The symbol is passed through, and loading now rejects duplicate symbols. I discarded the run and recorded why in the decision log.
Protection
A pipeline test asserts that each symbol is attributed separately.

12Next

The decisive experiment

Turning the synthetic machinery into evidence takes four steps, in this order:

  1. Replay one validated LOBSTER day through all eight worlds. Measure the real replay time and confirm the property tests hold on real flow.
  2. Commit and tag the pre-registration before computing any market attribution.
  3. Run the attribution on all five symbols with a search budget large enough for the benchmark policy to converge. Report adverse selection first, with bounds.
  4. Only if that attribution is stable, open the held-out data once to test the corrected rule.
